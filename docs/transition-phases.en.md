# Transition Phases: From Day One to Free Elections

> **In plain words**
>
> If the political system changes, things must be done in the right order: first people's safety and keeping water, electricity, bread and salaries flowing; then freeing political prisoners and restoring free speech; then writing a new constitution; and finally free elections.
> In the first days no one may take revenge and no office closes; employees keep their jobs.
> An interim council runs the country for a short time and has no right to stay.
> Within about a year people elect representatives to write the constitution, and within about two to three years everything is handed over to institutions elected by the people.
> All dates are approximate, but the deadline for the interim period is firm.

## What this document says and does not say

This document is the backbone of the whole transition plan. The [institutions](institutions/index.md) and [topics](topics/index.md) documents all refer to this timeline.

**"Day zero"** means the moment the current power structure can no longer, or will no longer, govern the country, and an interim transitional authority takes responsibility. That moment may come from a negotiated agreement, a collapse after nationwide protests, a split within the ruling system, or another crisis. Each scenario has different details, described in [regime change scenarios](topics/regime-change-scenarios.md) (Persian). This document is the path they all share.

In two scenarios the clock starts differently: in a **gradual transition from within**, day zero is the moment the existing structure formally accepts the process of electing a constituent assembly; in a scenario of **fragmentation and armed conflict**, day zero begins after a nationwide ceasefire.

This document does **not** predict when or how change will happen, and it prescribes no path to day zero other than peaceful ones. Its only subject is: **when that day comes, how do we keep the country whole?**

## Seven principles of sequencing

1. **Life first, then bread, then freedom, then law, then the vote.** Order matters. Elections held amid insecurity, or a constitution written in the excitement of the first days, are fragile.
2. **No legal vacuum.** All existing laws remain valid unless explicitly and publicly suspended (such as discriminatory laws, executions, censorship and internet filtering). This prevents legal chaos.
3. **No administrative vacuum.** All state employees stay in their jobs and are paid. Removing any individual is only possible through a defined, individual and appealable procedure.
4. **Justice starts on day one, but courts are not formed in haste.** In the first days, documents and evidence are preserved; trials take place after independent and fair courts exist.
5. **Every interim body has an expiry date.** The interim authority lasts at most 12 months, and the whole transition, until power is handed to institutions elected under a new constitution, lasts at most 30 months. The stage limits add up: constituent assembly elections by month 10, drafting at most 12 months, the referendum within 2 months and general elections within 4 months after that — 28 months, with a 2-month margin. **No unelected body may extend this limit.**
6. **Large and irreversible decisions are left to elected bodies.** The form of government, the constitution, the sale of major state assets and long-term foreign contracts cannot be finalized by the interim authority.
7. **Each phase ends by criteria, not only by the calendar.** Each phase has clear criteria for being "done". If they are not met, the time limits still stand, and instead of extensions, predefined remedies apply (see "If the plan falls behind").

## Who decides during the transition?

This is one of the most sensitive questions of any transition, because in the first months no institution has been chosen by a free vote. There are three main models:

**A) A temporary military council.** The army, or part of the armed forces, takes power temporarily and promises elections. Egypt (2011–2013) and Sudan (2019–2021) show that this model often ends in a return to military rule. [citation needed] **This plan rejects this model.** The armed forces have a vital role in maintaining security during the transition, but under civilian command.

**B) A single interim leader.** A well-known figure takes responsibility for the transition. The advantages are speed and clarity; the risks are concentration of power in one person, dependence of the whole transition on one individual, and the difficulty of stepping down voluntarily.

**C) A collective interim council with a technocratic government.** A small and diverse council takes political responsibility, and an interim government of competent managers and specialists handles day-to-day administration.

**My proposal: model C, with these safeguards:**

- **Composition:** the interim transitional council has 9 to 15 members and must reflect Iran's political, gender, ethnic, religious and regional diversity. At least one third of its members are women. No one credibly accused of direct involvement in serious crimes may be a member.
- **Commitment to the charter:** before starting, each member publicly signs the commitments of the [manifesto](manifesto.md) and of the transitional charter (red lines, fundamental rights, timeline).
- **Not running for office:** members of the interim council and the head of the interim government do not run in the constituent assembly elections or in the first election for the highest executive office. This removes the incentive to use temporary power to gain permanent power. (This proposal is contested; see "Open questions".)
- **Decision-making:** ordinary decisions by simple majority, important ones (such as declaring a state of emergency or suspending a law) by two-thirds. All decisions are published within 24 hours.
- **Accountability:** a broad "national advisory assembly" (representatives of professions, universities, provinces, minorities, workers, women and Iranians abroad) has the right to publicly question the council and the interim government.
- **Expiry:** the council's powers end automatically at the first session of the elected constituent assembly, and in any case at most 12 months after day zero.

How the council's members are chosen depends on the scenario: in a negotiated transition, through the negotiating table; in a collapse scenario, through an immediate agreement among political and civic forces inside and outside the country that have signed the charter, confirmed by a "national conference" in the first weeks. None of these routes is fully democratic; that is why the council's legitimacy comes only from three things: **inclusiveness, transparency and a short life.**

## Phase 0: Preparation (from today to day zero)

Most transition failures are decided in this phase: countries without a plan for "the day after" were caught unprepared on the day after. What can be done from today:

- **Writing and criticizing the plan.** This repository. Every document should have been criticized and corrected many times before day zero.
- **A register of skills.** Identify the roles and skills needed for each institution (not people's names; see [people needed](people-needed.md)) so that on day zero it is clear who is needed.
- **Drafts of decrees and texts.** The texts of first-day messages, a draft transitional charter, the list of laws to be suspended, and instructions for keeping offices running.
- **Building trust with institutions' employees.** A clear and repeated message to civil servants, teachers, police, army personnel and even ordinary members of military institutions: your place is secure, responsibility is individual, and no one will be punished for their job.
- **Documentation.** Safe and professional recording of evidence of human rights violations by independent bodies, in a form usable in court later.
- **Dialogue between groups.** Agreement among political forces on the minimum: red lines, the timeline and the interim decision-making mechanism. Without this agreement, day zero becomes a violent competition for power.
- **Preparing Iranians abroad.** A register of expert volunteers, readiness for remote technical help and a return program (see [diaspora](topics/diaspora.md), Persian).

**Readiness criterion:** for each vital task of the first 72 hours, there is a ready text, a defined role and a fallback plan.

## Phase 1: The first 72 hours — immediate stability

**Goal:** no one is killed, no infrastructure fails, no document is destroyed, and people know what is happening.

### Twelve vital tasks

1. **An order to stop violence and a "no revenge" message.** The interim authority's first public message: all armed forces return to barracks; anyone who lays down arms and does not fire on people is safe and keeps their salary; no person or group may punish another, and every accusation will be handled only in court.
2. **Security in the cities.** The police (except units directly involved in repression) stay on duty with a clear order: "protect people, don't repress them". Volunteer neighborhood committees operate only unarmed, and only to help and report.
3. **Protecting critical infrastructure.** Power plants and the grid, water facilities, refineries and fuel depots, telecommunications, ports and airports, hospitals, grain silos and bakeries, medicine warehouses, and bank payment systems. For each, current technical managers stay unless a replacement is ready. See [essential services](topics/essential-services.md) (Persian).
4. **Securing nuclear facilities and weapons depots.** Nuclear facilities and materials and radioactive sources are protected, and the International Atomic Energy Agency is informed. Arsenals and ammunition depots are sealed and guarded so weapons do not spread among groups; Libya in 2011 showed that looted arsenals can bring years of insecurity. [citation needed]
5. **Protecting archives and evidence.** The archives of security and intelligence bodies, prison records, institutions' financial records and mass grave sites must be protected from destruction and looting. These documents are vital both for justice and for preventing rumors and revenge. In East Germany, citizens occupied the offices of the secret police (Stasi) in December 1989 and January 1990 to stop the destruction of files. [citation needed]
6. **Prisons.** Prisons are protected, not thrown open. The release of political prisoners and prisoners of conscience begins as fast as possible on the basis of verified lists, but releasing all prisoners indiscriminately would endanger the public. The lives of all prisoners, including detained officials of the former system, are protected.
7. **Salaries and payments.** Official announcement: all salaries, pensions and cash subsidies will be paid as usual. Banks stay open with temporary limits on withdrawals and foreign-currency transfers to prevent bank runs and capital flight.
8. **Information.** One official, verifiable channel for announcing decisions; a daily briefing to the public; internet access restored as fast as possible. In days of crisis, rumor kills more than bullets.
9. **Announcing the interim authority and its commitments.** The members of the interim transitional council, their public commitments, the deadline and the first steps. **The council's first resolution** suspends the powers of the unelected supervisory bodies (such as the Guardian Council and the Expediency Council), halts all executions and corporal punishments (such as flogging and amputation), and declares that all other laws and offices continue to function. The transitional charter later formalizes this resolution.
10. **Protecting minorities and places of worship.** Mosques, Hosseiniyehs, churches, synagogues, fire temples and other religious sites, as well as clerics and their families, are protected from attack. Regime change must not turn into revenge against religion or religious people.
11. **A message to neighbors and the world.** Iran in transition threatens no country; borders are respected; we welcome humanitarian and technical help, not military intervention.
12. **Health care.** Hospitals and emergency services work at full capacity; reserves of critical medicines and blood are checked and secured.

### What not to do in the first 72 hours

- **Dissolve any institution.** Dissolution is a considered decision taken in later phases, with a plan.
- **Mass dismissals.** Only commanders and managers with a clear direct role in repression are removed, and their replacements come from the clean deputies and staff of the same institution.
- **Summary courts.** No court is formed in the first days to "try" officials. Detention only by order of a judicial authority, and only to prevent flight or destruction of evidence.
- **Arbitrary confiscation.** Assets of suspect institutions and individuals are frozen and placed under trustee management only by court order — not handed to anyone.
- **Unrealistic economic promises.** Promises such as "prices will drop immediately" or "oil money on every table" that cannot be kept destroy trust within weeks.

### Success criteria for the first 72 hours

- No organized armed fighting continues in the cities.
- Electricity, water, bread, fuel and medicine are available in every province.
- No major archive has been destroyed or looted.
- People know who the interim authority is, what it has promised and where to get accurate news.

## Phase 2: The first 30 days — establishment

**Goal:** the administration of the country gets back on track, basic freedoms are established, and the economy is saved from free fall.

**Governance.** An interim technocratic government is formed from experienced and reputable managers and specialists, from inside and outside the institutions; the criteria are competence, integrity and commitment to the charter, not factional loyalty. Under the **principle of continued service**, all state employees keep their jobs; for senior managers, a "temporary review" procedure is announced — only those facing specific and credible accusations are suspended from management duties until their case is clarified (with base pay continued). Governors directly involved in repression are replaced; the rest of the provincial structure continues, with provincial advisory councils of local representatives. A national conference and a national advisory assembly are convened.

**Freedoms and rights.** All political prisoners and prisoners of conscience are released, and all cases related to speech, belief, dress, trade-union activity and peaceful civic activity are dropped. Executions and corporal punishments remain halted until elected institutions decide. Compulsory hijab enforcement ends and "morality police" units are dissolved. Censorship laws are suspended and internet filtering is lifted; only direct incitement to violence remains prosecutable. Parties, unions and associations operate freely, provided they renounce violence and weapons. Iranians abroad may return freely, and all cases opened only because of peaceful political activity or emigration are dropped.

**Security.** Commanders of the army, the IRGC and the police report to the interim civilian authority; commanders who refuse, or who had a direct role in repression, are replaced. IRGC units remain in their barracks; the paramilitary and street activities of the Basij stop; members' pay continues until a final plan is announced. A voluntary program collects weapons held outside arsenals, with no prosecution for handing them in.

**Economy.** An emergency economic program secures basic goods, fuel and medicine, continues subsidies, and sets a transparent exchange-rate policy to halt the collapse of the rial. The assets of large quasi-state economic conglomerates are frozen by court order and placed under trustee management to prevent asset flight — not hasty confiscation. Talks begin on lifting sanctions and releasing Iran's frozen assets abroad.

**Justice.** An "evidence and archives preservation board" is formed under independent judges and lawyers. People can register complaints about human rights violations; investigation comes later, but registration starts now. The draft transitional charter is published for public consultation.

### Success criteria for the first 30 days

- The interim government is complete and all ministries are working.
- Salaries and pensions have been paid on time at least once.
- All known political prisoners have been released.
- The internet is available without filtering.
- The exchange rate and the prices of basic goods are no longer in free fall.

## Phase 3: Months 2 to 6 — foundations

**Goal:** the rules of the democratic game are written, and the institutions needed for free elections, justice and institutional reform are built.

- **The transitional charter** (a short interim constitution) is adopted by the national conference after public consultation. It includes fundamental rights, the red lines, the structure of interim institutions, the timeline and **foundational principles that the future constitution may not violate** (such as equal rights, judicial independence, and free and periodic elections). This model comes from South Africa, whose 1993 interim constitution set 34 principles for the final constitution. [citation needed] Interim laws on political parties and on the media follow.
- **Elections:** an independent electoral commission is established with non-partisan members, its own budget and international observation; the voter register is updated and a voting mechanism for Iranians abroad is designed; a constituent assembly election law based on proportional representation ensures that all currents and regions are represented.
- **Transitional justice:** a transitional justice law creates a truth commission, a special prosecutor's office for serious crimes, a reparations fund for victims, and individual vetting of personnel.
- **Institutional reform:** a security sector reform plan (integration, retraining and DDR — disarmament, demobilization and reintegration — for paramilitary forces, and a final plan for the IRGC); judicial independence (an interim high judicial council and vetting of judges); an independent anti-corruption body and an asset recovery program.
- **Economy:** a transparent transition budget; operational independence of the central bank and an inflation-control program; a subsidy reform plan with cash compensation for low-income households, implemented only once stability is established.

### Success criteria for month 6

- The transitional charter has been adopted and published.
- The independent electoral commission exists and the date of the constituent assembly election is announced.
- The transitional justice law has been adopted and the truth commission has started work.
- No armed group outside state command controls any city.

## Phase 4: Month 6 to about month 24 — a new constitution

**Goal:** people elect representatives who write a new constitution with broad public participation.

1. **Constituent assembly elections** between month 6 and month 10 at the latest — the first fully free national election. In provinces where the independent electoral commission certifies readiness, **interim local councils** may be elected at the same time, so that cities and villages gain electoral legitimacy sooner.
2. **The end of the interim council.** At the first session of the constituent assembly, the interim council's powers end. Besides writing the constitution, the constituent assembly approves or appoints the interim government and passes only urgent laws.
3. **Drafting the constitution** within at most 12 months of the constituent assembly's first session (target: 10 months), with public sessions in every province and in several languages, the right of every citizen (including Iranians abroad) to submit proposals, and publication of all drafts and minutes.
4. **The question of the form of government.** Whether Iran is a republic or a constitutional monarchy, and whether its system is parliamentary or presidential, is among the most important decisions of this phase. **No referendum on the form of government is held in the first six months,** so that people have time for free and informed debate. The referendum of March 1979 (Farvardin 1358), held only two months after the revolution with a single "yes or no" option, is an example that must not be repeated. [citation needed] Options and my proposal are in the [constitution](topics/constitution.md) document (Persian).
5. **Certification.** An interim constitutional court or an independent legal panel checks that the final draft complies with the foundational principles of the transitional charter.
6. **Constitutional referendum** at most two months after adoption by the constituent assembly, under domestic and international observation.

**Success criterion:** the new constitution has been approved in a free referendum with acceptable participation in every province. (Very low participation in part of the country is a serious warning about legitimacy; Iraq's experience in 2005 showed that a constitution rejected by a large part of one community lays the ground for later conflict. [citation needed])

## Phase 5: About month 24 to month 30 at the latest — free elections and handover

- **General elections** for the institutions foreseen in the new constitution (parliament, and if foreseen, the head of state), at most four months after the referendum. Preparations (voter register, districts, observers) start while the constitution is being drafted so that this window is enough.
- **Local elections** (city and village councils and, if foreseen, provincial councils).
- **A formal and public handover** from the interim government to the elected government.
- **Dissolution of all interim bodies** that have no place in the new constitution.

**Success criterion:** the first peaceful transfer of power to institutions elected under the new constitution has taken place.

## Phase 6: Years 3 to 5 — consolidation

Democracy is not built with a single election. Scholars of transitions usually consider a democracy "consolidated" when all major actors see democracy as "the only game in town" and power has changed hands peacefully between rivals at least twice. [citation needed] The tasks of this period: completing the truth commission's work and the trials; completing security sector reform and the integration of forces; structural economic reform and anti-corruption; implementing decentralization; and civic education and strengthening civil society and independent media.

## Safeguards: what prevents extension and deviation?

- **Time limits in the transitional charter** that can only be changed by referendum.
- **Interim council members do not run** in the first elections.
- **Full transparency:** publication of all decisions, budgets and contracts.
- **Independent oversight:** civil society, free media, international election observers.
- **The right to protest:** peaceful protest is free at every stage.
- **This repository:** any deviation from this plan can be measured against these documents; the [transition scorecard](scorecard.md) lists the criteria.

**One clear exception:** the truth commission and the special prosecutor are not interim governing bodies, and their work may continue past month 30, because fair justice takes time. Their continuation must be confirmed by the first elected parliament.

## If the plan falls behind

No plan runs exactly on schedule. The general rule is: **delay is allowed; extending interim power is not.**

- **If security does not allow constituent assembly elections everywhere by month 10:** elections are held in the provinces that are secure, and the assembly convenes once at least two-thirds of its seats are filled; other seats are filled as soon as possible. The interim council's life never exceeds 12 months, **under any circumstances**.
- **If the constituent assembly has not written the constitution within 12 months:** one two-month extension by a two-thirds vote is allowed (the same two-month margin within the 30-month limit); after that, the assembly is dissolved and a new one is elected. Tunisia's constituent assembly took much longer than originally planned, and the delay fed public distrust. [citation needed]
- **If an exceptional event occurs** (war, a large-scale disaster, or insecurity that makes elections impossible): only the **elected constituent assembly** may, by a two-thirds vote, once, and by at most six months, move the 30-month limit; no unelected body has that right.
- **If the constitution is rejected in the referendum:** this is not failure but the people's message. The constituent assembly presents a revised draft within six months; this is the only case in which the 30-month limit moves automatically, by those six months, because the people themselves made that decision in the referendum. Chile rejected two draft constitutions in referendums in 2022 and 2023, and its political system still held. [citation needed]

## Open questions for public discussion

- Should interim council members be barred from running forever, or only in the first elections? Would the bar discourage capable people from taking on interim responsibility?
- How should the interim council be chosen to have the greatest legitimacy?
- Is 30 months the right limit? Some say it is too short to build institutions; others say every month of unelected government is dangerous.
- How should the referendum on the form of government and the drafting of the constitution be sequenced?
- How should Iranians abroad take part in constituent assembly elections — in domestic constituencies or through dedicated seats?
