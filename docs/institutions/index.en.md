# What happens to institutions?

> **In plain words**
>
> A country works through its institutions: courts, the army, banks, hospitals, schools, municipalities and dozens of other bodies.
> This plan dissolves no institution overnight and punishes no employee for their job.
> What serves people is preserved and reformed; what was built only for repression is dismantled — with a plan, and without leaving a vacuum.
> Each institution has its own document (in Persian) describing its current state and what happens to it in the transition; below is an English summary of each.

## A message to everyone who works in Iran's institutions

If you work in one of these institutions — as a clerk, teacher, nurse, police officer, soldier, judge or bank employee:

- **Your job is secure.** Under the **principle of continued service**, all state employees keep their jobs and salaries.
- **Responsibility is individual, not collective.** No one is punished for their uniform, title or workplace. Only those who personally committed crimes answer for them, and only before a fair court.
- **Vetting is individual, with a right of defense.** Only senior managers, or people facing a specific and credible accusation, are reviewed — with the right to defend themselves and to appeal, and with base pay continuing meanwhile.
- **Your pension is secure,** including the pensions of armed forces retirees.
- **Your knowledge is needed.** No transition succeeds without the specialists who run these institutions today.

The full documents are in Persian. [Help translate them](../participate.md).

## Security and justice

**[The judiciary](judiciary.md).** People rely on the courts for divorce, inheritance, property deeds and complaints; that work must not stop for a single day. Today judges are not independent, some courts exist specifically for political opponents, and coerced confessions and frequent executions have been common. In the transition, ordinary courts continue, all executions are halted immediately, and the Revolutionary Courts and the Special Clerical Court are dismantled. Judges with clean records stay, each judge is reviewed individually rather than collectively, and past crimes are tried in fair courts — not through revenge.

**[Prisons and detention centers](prisons.md).** On the day of change, prison gates are not thrown open; prisons are protected so that no one escapes and no one is harmed. Political prisoners and prisoners of conscience are released on the basis of verified lists in the first days and weeks. The lives of all prisoners — including detained former officials — are protected, and no one has the right to revenge. Prison staff keep their jobs unless personally involved in abusing prisoners; afterwards prisons become more humane and less crowded, with alternatives to prison for minor offenses.

**[The police](police.md).** The police must stay on duty from the first hour, because without them theft and insecurity rise and people feel forced to arm themselves. But their order changes: protect people, don't beat protesters or arrest anyone over the hijab. The "morality police" are dissolved and commanders directly involved in repression step aside, while ordinary officers and conscripts stay. The police come under the civilian Interior Ministry, and people can complain about any officer to an independent body; neighborhood volunteer groups operate only unarmed, only to help and report.

**[Intelligence and security agencies](intelligence.md).** Today several separate intelligence bodies spy on citizens, activists and even Iranians abroad, and none answers to elected representatives. On the day of change, the most important task is to ensure their files are neither destroyed nor stolen, because the truth about prisoners, the disappeared and victims lies in them. Spying on citizens for their beliefs and political activity stops immediately, and these bodies are merged into one small civilian agency limited to terrorism, foreign espionage and major organized crime, with no power of arrest and under parliamentary and judicial oversight. Each employee is reviewed individually, and victims may, under clear rules, see their own files.

**[The army (Artesh)](army.md).** The army defends Iran's borders and territory and must keep doing only that in the transition — not confront protesters, and not seize power itself. No soldier may fire on the people, and no officer is punished for their uniform; pay continues as always. The army comes under a civilian defense minister, with elected representatives overseeing its budget and work. Politics and ideology leave the barracks, only senior commanders are individually reviewed, and the future of conscription is put to public debate and decided by elected representatives.

**[The IRGC and the Basij](irgc-basij.md).** The Revolutionary Guards and the Basij are large armed forces with roles in the economy and politics, yet most of their members are ordinary people with families to support. Dissolving them overnight would make the country unsafe, but keeping them as they are is incompatible with democracy. The proposal is a planned, phased disaggregation: regular military units join a unified national army after individual vetting; external operations and paramilitary activity end; their companies are audited under court supervision. Anyone who does not fire on the people and hands in weapons as planned keeps their life and livelihood; only those who personally committed crimes answer in a fair court, and final decisions rest with elected representatives.

## Governance and politics

**[The Leader's office and unelected councils](leadership-councils.md).** Under the current system, many major decisions are made by the Supreme Leader and several councils that the people did not freely elect, or whose candidates were pre-screened. From day zero, the powers of these bodies are suspended, but their staff keep working and their records are carefully preserved. Necessary functions — such as reviewing laws or security coordination — pass to interim and then elected bodies. The lives and dignity of clerics and officials of these bodies are protected, and any accusation is handled only by a fair court; what, if anything, replaces these councils is decided by the people.

**[Parliament (Majlis)](parliament.md).** Today's parliament is elected, but only from candidates approved by the Guardian Council, and every law it passes must pass the Guardian Council's filter again. In the transition, the current parliament is temporarily suspended from law-making, while its staff, research center and library are preserved and no member is punished for their votes. Until the constituent assembly meets, only urgent laws are made, after public consultation and with expiry dates; then the elected constituent assembly both writes the constitution and passes essential laws. The future parliament must be transparent: every member's votes published, assets declared, and real power to hold the government to account.

**[The electoral system](elections.md).** Today the Interior Ministry runs elections and the Guardian Council decides who may run, which is why many people no longer trust the ballot box. In the transition, an independent, non-partisan commission takes over, and no body may exclude candidates for their beliefs. Votes are cast on paper and counted the same night, at the polling station, in front of candidates' representatives and observers. Iranians abroad can vote, and domestic and international observers see every stage; the first free election is for the constituent assembly, with a system that gives every current, province, women and minorities a voice.

**[The executive and civil service](civil-service.md).** Civil servants are the backbone of the state: without them no salary is paid and no file moves. The plan's core rule is simple: every employee stays in their job and is paid. Only senior managers, or those facing specific and credible complaints, are reviewed — one by one, with the right to defense and appeal. Ideological screening in hiring is abolished; hiring will be based on merit alone, and over the following years offices become more efficient and digital without anyone losing their job overnight.

**[The Interior Ministry and provincial governments](governorates.md).** The Interior Ministry and governors' offices are how the central government runs Iran's 31 provinces; today governors are appointed from Tehran without any say by local people. On the day of change, each province must immediately have a known interim official so that water, power, bread and security continue. Running elections is taken from the Interior Ministry and given to an independent commission. In the long term, people in each province elect representatives to run their province, without harming Iran's unity.

**[Municipalities and local councils](municipalities.md).** Municipalities collect waste, fight fires, run buses and metros and keep parks — none of which may stop for a day. City and village councils are elected, but many candidates are disqualified beforehand, and municipalities earn much of their income by selling extra building permits, which has damaged cities. In the transition, mayors stay unless personally involved in repression or corruption. The long-term goal is for residents of each city and village to decide on their local budget and priorities, transparently.

**[Diplomacy and the Foreign Ministry](foreign-affairs.md).** Iran has professional, experienced diplomats, but its foreign policy has often been set by bodies other than the Foreign Ministry, keeping the country isolated and sanctioned for years. In the first days, Iran tells its neighbors and the world that it threatens no one, respects borders and welcomes humanitarian aid — but not foreign military intervention. Foreign embassies in Tehran are protected, and Iran's embassies and consulates keep serving Iranians abroad. The new foreign policy rests on good neighborliness, non-interference, trade and human rights, and support for armed groups in other countries ends; major decisions, such as relations with the United States or Israel, belong to the elected government, not the interim one.

## Economy and energy

**[The central bank and banking system](central-bank.md).** The central bank issues the national currency and supervises banks, but it is not independent today, and printing money to cover government and bank deficits is a main cause of chronic inflation. In the first days, bank cards, ATMs and salary deposits must keep working without interruption. Deposits are guaranteed and, if necessary, public and temporary limits are set on withdrawals and foreign-currency transfers to prevent capital flight. In the following months the central bank gains legal independence, all banks' books are reviewed and multiple exchange rates are unified; bank employees stay, and only those who personally broke the rules are held accountable.

**[Public finance: budget, taxes and customs](public-finance.md).** The state needs money to pay teachers, nurses and pensioners, and it comes from oil, taxes, customs and borrowing. Today the budget depends on oil, some wealthy institutions pay no tax, and parts of revenue and spending are hidden from the public. In the first days of the transition, the priority is paying everyone's salaries and pensions on time. In the first month a simple, public emergency budget is published, and by month six a full, transparent budget that ends tax exemptions for the commercial arms of foundations; over the following years taxes become fairer and oil dependence lower.

**[Oil and the energy sector](oil-energy.md).** Oil and gas are Iran's greatest natural wealth, and most electricity is generated from gas. Yet gas runs short in winter, power cuts come in summer, cheap fuel is smuggled out and oil revenue has never been fully transparent. In the first days, refineries, pipelines and power plants are protected and specialists stay at work so that fuel, gas and electricity keep flowing. All oil revenue goes into one public account so people can see what each barrel sold for and where the money went; energy prices do not jump overnight, and any reform is gradual, announced in advance and paired with cash payments to households.

**[Foundations (bonyads) and quasi-state conglomerates](bonyads.md).** The foundations are very wealthy organizations owning factories, land, companies and banks, yet they answer to no elected body and many pay little or no tax. Some do real welfare work — for instance helping poor families or the families of those killed and disabled in war — and in the transition that help does not stop for a single day, and their employees keep their jobs. Their assets are placed by court order under independent trustees so that no one can smuggle them abroad or loot them. After full audits, the people decide, through elected representatives, how this wealth returns to the whole nation.

**[Social security and pension funds](social-security.md).** The Social Security Organization and pension funds pay millions of pensioners and cover many workers' health and unemployment insurance; many are in deficit, depend on state support, and pensions have lagged behind inflation. From the first hour, it is announced that all pensions will be paid — including those of army and IRGC retirees. The funds' accounts and companies are then audited, and representatives of workers and pensioners gain a real role in running them. Any change to retirement age or contributions happens only after public debate and a decision by elected representatives.

**[Atomic energy and the nuclear program](atomic-energy.md).** Iran has a nuclear power plant, several enrichment and research sites, and units producing radioactive materials for medicine and industry; the program has been a main cause of sanctions and tension, and in recent years was even the target of military strikes. In the first days, the priority is keeping nuclear and radioactive materials — including those in hospitals and factories — secure and out of anyone's hands. Scientists and engineers keep their jobs and nuclear medicine continues. The proposal is a voluntary suspension of enrichment during the transition and full access for inspectors, with the final decision on the program's future left to the elected government.

## Society and services

**[Health care](health.md).** Hospitals, emergency services, pharmacies and rural health houses must not close for even an hour. Iran's health system has real strengths, such as its rural primary care network, but struggles with medicine shortages, emigration of doctors and nurses and heavy out-of-pocket costs. Every wounded person, from any side, must be treated without discrimination, and no health worker may be harmed for treating anyone. Safe channels for medicine imports open in the first months, and in later years the aim is that no family is impoverished by illness and that the psychological wounds of years of violence are treated too.

**[Water and the environment](water-environment.md).** Iran faces a serious water crisis: aquifers are being depleted, land is subsiding in many plains, and lakes and wetlands have dried up. On the day of change, the first task is uninterrupted safe drinking water and protecting dams and water facilities. Then the real water data must be told to the public, because the right path cannot be found without the truth. Agricultural water use must fall, but not at the cost of farmers' jobs and poverty; since water disputes can turn into local conflicts in the transition, decisions must be fair, transparent and made with each region's participation.

**[Education](education.md).** Iran's schools today carry heavy ideological content, teachers are poorly paid and children in deprived provinces receive poor education. Schools may close for a few days for safety, but reopen quickly. No teacher is dismissed for having taught under the old system, and all teachers are paid on time. Units that monitored the beliefs of pupils and teachers are dismantled, and teachers, parents and experts reform the curriculum calmly together; in future, wherever communities wish, children also learn in their mother tongue alongside Persian.

**[Universities and research](universities.md).** Iran's universities have capable faculty and students, but ideological screening, security forces on campus and the expulsion of critical students and professors have long limited their freedom. Campus security is maintained, and a path opens for expelled students and dismissed professors to have their cases reviewed and return. Within six months ideological screening is abolished and a law guarantees university independence and academic freedom. No professor is dismissed for their beliefs — even if they defended the old system — because a "cultural revolution" in reverse must not happen.

**[Broadcasting and the media](media.md).** Radio and television are a monopoly of one organization whose head is appointed by the Leader, and it has repeatedly aired confessions extracted from prisoners under pressure; newspapers, books, films and music need state permission, and satellite dishes are banned. On the day of change, state broadcasting is not switched off; it airs only accurate, neutral news, and no one takes revenge on its journalists and staff. Within a month censorship ends, the press is freed and owning a satellite dish is no longer a crime. State broadcasting then becomes an independent public broadcaster belonging to all the people, broadcasting in Iran's many languages.

**[Telecommunications and the internet](telecom-internet.md).** The internet and phone networks are under tight state control: many sites and apps are filtered, and the internet has been repeatedly shut down during protests. On the day of change, networks stay on, are protected from sabotage, and any shutdown ends immediately. Within a month filtering is lifted, and data collected on citizens is preserved under judicial control for legal proceedings, with no one allowed to publish it. A law then bans wiretapping without a judge's warrant and protects privacy; telecom staff keep their jobs because the country needs their knowledge.

**[Religious institutions and seminaries](religious-institutions.md).** Religion and state are separated in both directions: the state does not control religion, and religion does not control the state. Clerics, mosques and seminaries are protected from revenge from day one, and every religious community — Shia, Sunni and all other faiths — is free to run its own institutions. State funding of propaganda bodies is phased out transparently, and religious endowments are managed openly according to their founders' intent.
