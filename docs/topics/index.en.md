# Cross-cutting topics

> **In plain words**
>
> Some issues belong to no single institution and decide the fate of the whole transition: justice for victims, a new constitution, the rights of women and of ethnic communities, security, the economy and sanctions.
> In each document, different views are presented fairly, the plan's proposal is given with its reasons, and the final decision is left to the people's vote.
> Two documents deal with experience: lessons from Iran's own history and from other countries.
> The full documents are in Persian; below is an English summary of each.

## Justice and rights

**[Transitional justice and national reconciliation](transitional-justice.md).** Over recent decades grave crimes were committed in Iran, and many families still don't know how their loved ones were killed or where they are buried. The proposal: those who ordered or committed the gravest crimes are tried in fair courts — not in the street and not in revenge. A truth commission publicly records what happened, and those with lesser offenses who tell the whole truth may be pardoned. Victims receive reparations, sites such as mass graves become memorials, and no one is punished for their job or family; the aim is that this suffering never recurs.

**[Principles of a new constitution and how to write it](constitution.md).** The constitution is the basic contract among all Iranians and must be written patiently, with everyone's participation and approved by a free vote — not in the excitement of the first days. First a short charter locks in a few foundational principles, such as equal rights and free elections; then elected representatives write the full text and the people accept or reject it in a referendum. The document proposes a government accountable to an elected parliament, with a purely symbolic head of state, so that power is never again concentrated in one person. Whether Iran becomes a republic or a constitutional monarchy the AI author deliberately does not choose: the people decide after months of free debate, on a clear ballot, and whichever option wins must guarantee every citizen's rights equally.

**[Civil liberties and human rights](civil-liberties.md).** In the future Iran no one should be imprisoned for their words, writing, beliefs or peaceful protest. From the first weeks, political prisoners are freed, censorship and filtering are lifted and people can freely assemble and organize. Torture and forced confessions are banned forever — even for those who themselves tortured. These freedoms are guarded by courts and independent institutions, not only by promises; on contested issues such as the death penalty, the document gives its proposal (abolition) with reasons, leaving the final decision to the people.

**[Women's rights](women-rights.md).** Women in Iran today have fewer legal rights than men in marriage, divorce, custody, inheritance, travel and even dress. The plan proposes ending compulsory dress rules in the first weeks — no one may force a woman either to wear or to remove the hijab. Equality of women and men is written into the transitional charter and the new constitution, and family law is reformed after broad debate by elected representatives. Clear laws, safe houses and trained police are needed against domestic violence, child marriage and so-called honor killings, and women must be present equally in work, government and the writing of the constitution.

**[Freedom of religion and belief](religious-freedom.md).** Followers of many faiths and people with no religion live side by side in Iran, but today's law does not treat them equally. The proposal is a state with no official religion that protects all beliefs equally and does not interfere in religion. Everyone is free to be religious, to change religion or to have none, without losing any right. Regime change must not become revenge against clerics, mosques or believers; all places of worship are protected, and the final decision on religion and state belongs to the people.

**[Rights of ethnic and linguistic communities, and decentralization](ethnic-rights-decentralization.md).** Iran is home to peoples of many languages and cultures, many of whom have long felt unheard. The proposal: people in each province elect representatives who make real decisions about local budgets and affairs, and every child can learn their mother tongue at school alongside Persian where the community wishes. Iran is not partitioned, but no region should be subordinate to another. Whether Iran ultimately becomes federal is decided by elected representatives and a free vote.

## Security and scenarios

**[Regime change scenarios](regime-change-scenarios.md).** No one knows when or how change will come to Iran, and this document makes no predictions. It examines seven possible paths — from negotiation to collapse and war — so as to be prepared for each. The plan supports neither violence nor foreign military attack; it only aims to keep the country whole and people safe whatever happens. Other countries' experience shows that the path to change strongly shapes its outcome, and some "no-regret" actions help in every scenario and should start today.

**[Security and preventing civil war](security-and-civil-war-prevention.md).** The most dangerous days of any regime change are those when no one knows who holds the weapons. The proposal: all armed forces remain under one command and a civilian authority, and no force is dissolved overnight. Every soldier, guard and police officer who lays down arms and does not fire on the people keeps their life and pay, while those who committed serious crimes answer in court. Dialogue is open to any armed group willing to renounce violence, and no such group is excluded from politics; the best guarantee of security is that every community feels the future Iran is its home.

**[Continuity of essential services](essential-services.md).** Water, electricity, gas, bread, medicine, fuel and money must reach people without interruption even in the hardest days. The workers and technical managers who run these services today stay in their jobs and are paid. Services depend on each other — if power fails, water pumps, hospitals, phones and card readers fail too — so power and fuel come first. A crisis staff in each province reports daily and gives special attention to the elderly, patients, people with disabilities and remote villages; hoarding is fought with sufficient supply, transparency and cash support, not sweeping price controls that create queues and black markets.

**[Public communication and countering rumors](public-communication.md).** In the first days of change, a rumor can be deadlier than a bullet: it can drive people to the banks, empty shelves or trigger attacks on innocents. So the interim authority needs a single official channel whose every message is numbered and signed, with a daily briefing at a fixed hour. If the internet is down, messages go out by radio, TV, SMS, printed notices, mosques and local councils, in Iran's many languages. The answer to lies is the truth, not censorship; bad news is told honestly too, and the document includes ready-to-adapt messages for the first 72 hours.

## Economy

**[The transition economy](transition-economy.md).** In the first days, the most important economic task is that salaries, pensions, subsidies, bread, medicine and fuel reach people without interruption. The plan makes no promise of "instant cheapness", because a broken promise destroys trust. Things happen in order: first halt the rial's fall and inflation, then gradually reform prices and rules, then restructure the economy. Prices of essentials like gasoline and bread do not change without notice and without cash support for low-income families, and all decisions, budgets and contracts are public.

**[Sanctions and returning to the global financial system](sanctions-and-global-finance.md).** Sanctions have made ordinary Iranians' lives harder: medicine is expensive and scarce, aircraft are aging and banks have no normal links with the world. After a change of system, sanctions do not vanish automatically, because each rests on a law or decision of a country or the UN. The proposal: first open channels for medicine, food and humanitarian aid, then lift sanctions step by step in return for clear, verifiable actions. Iran's frozen assets abroad must return with full transparency under independent oversight, and major agreements are approved by elected institutions.

**[Fighting corruption and recovering assets](anti-corruption.md).** Corruption in Iran is not the work of a few individuals; it is rooted in a system that hands some people cheap currency, loans, contracts and exemptions and hides everything from the public. The proposal: an independent anti-corruption body, public asset declarations for officials, full transparency of budgets and contracts, and protection for whistleblowers. Illegally acquired assets are frozen and returned only by court order and due process, never by arbitrary confiscation, and anti-corruption must never become a tool of political revenge. Recovering money taken abroad is possible but takes years, so preparation must start now.

## History and experience

**[Lessons from other transitions](lessons-from-transitions.md).** Many countries have moved from dictatorship or war toward popular government; some succeeded, others sank into chaos and war. This document examines what each did, what worked and what failed — Spain, South Africa, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Chile, Indonesia, Tunisia, Iraq, Libya, Syria and others. The key lessons are simple: don't dissolve the army and administration overnight, avoid revenge, agree on the rules before competing for power, and keep the interim period short. No country is like Iran, so each lesson says why it does or doesn't apply; the biggest lesson for Iranians is Iran's own experience in 1979.

**[Lessons from Iran's modern history](iran-history-lessons.md).** Iranians have struggled for freedom and the rule of law for more than a century and have come close several times. Each time a door opened it closed again, because laws existed on paper while independent courts, an army bound by law and agreement among groups did not. The document reviews each period, from the 1906 Constitutional Revolution to today, and draws lessons for the day of change; the greatest is that revolution without a plan, and revenge instead of justice, produce a new despotism. Judgments are about events and structures, not individuals.

**[Return and participation of the diaspora](diaspora.md).** Millions of Iranians live abroad, with knowledge, experience and capital that can help rebuild Iran [citation needed]. Every Iranian who wishes should be able to return without fear, vote and work, and no one should be punished for having emigrated. But those returning come to help, not to rule; power comes only through the people's vote. Clear, fair rules are needed for voting from abroad, recognizing foreign degrees, military service and confiscated property; the aim is for Iranians inside and outside to become one nation again, not two camps.
